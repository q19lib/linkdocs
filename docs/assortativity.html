    Network structure

    Assortativity

    Whether well-connected nodes link to other well-connected nodes. Social networks tend to be assortative; the web, where hubs collect links from countless small sites, tends to be disassortative.

    Assortativity measures whether nodes link to nodes like themselves. The usual version compares degrees: in an assortative graph, high-degree nodes tend to link to other high-degree nodes; in a disassortative graph, hubs link mostly to low-degree nodes. Mark Newman introduced the standard coefficient in 2002 and found a broad pattern: social networks tend to be assortative, while technological and information networks, the web among them, tend to be disassortative.

    Definition

    The degree assortativity rrr is the Pearson correlation between the degrees at the two ends of an arc, taken over all arcs. On a directed graph there are four versions, pairing the source’s in- or out-degree with the target’s in- or out-degree. The natural one for link graphs pairs the linking node’s out-degree with the linked node’s in-degree:

    r=corr⁡(u,v)∈A(d+(u), d−(v)).r = \operatorname{corr}_{(u,v) \in A}\bigl(d^+(u),\, d^-(v)\bigr) .r=corr(u,v)∈A​(d+(u),d−(v)).

    rrr lies between −1 and +1; 0 means degrees at the two ends are unrelated, as in the configuration model (see random graph models).

    Worked example

    Over the ten arcs of the running example:

    Pairing (source, target) rrr
    out-degree, in-degree −0.34
    in-degree, in-degree −0.21
    out-degree, out-degree −0.21
    in-degree, out-degree −0.22
    undirected degrees (9 edges) −0.44

    Every version is negative. E, the node with the most out-links, links to D and F, which have one in-link each, while the most-linked node, C, receives links from nodes of every kind.

    Why the web is disassortative

    Popular sites receive links from enormous numbers of small sites, and small sites rarely receive links from popular ones. Infrastructure domains embedded on millions of pages (see Common Crawl web graph) are the extreme case. Link farms push the other way: a farm of domains that all link to each other is a group of nodes with similar, often high, degrees, a locally assortative pocket in a disassortative graph (see link spam).

    Measuring it on heavy-tailed graphs

    Pearson’s rrr is dominated by the few largest degrees, and Litvak and van der Hofstad showed that on large scale-free graphs it converges towards zero whatever the actual mixing. Rank-based versions, such as Spearman’s correlation of the degrees at the two ends of each arc (see Spearman’s rho), or correlations of log-degrees, are more reliable for web-scale graphs.
