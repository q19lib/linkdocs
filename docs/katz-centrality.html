    Centrality

    Katz centrality

    Count every walk that ends at a node, weighting a walk of length k by β to the power k. Short walks count most, everyone gets a baseline, and one parameter slides the measure from in-degree to eigenvector centrality.

    Leo Katz proposed in 1953 to measure a person’s status by counting not only who chooses them directly but also who chooses those people, and so on, with each extra step counting less. On a link graph, Katz centrality counts every walk that ends at a node, weighting walks of length kkk by βk\beta^kβk for some attenuation factor 0<β<10 < \beta < 10<β<1. It sits between Degree centrality, which counts walks of length 1, and Eigenvector centrality, which it approaches as β\betaβ grows. It also fixes eigenvector centrality’s habit of giving most of the web a score of zero.

    Definition

    Since (Ak)uv(A^k)_{uv}(Ak)uv​ counts the walks of length kkk from uuu to vvv (see Adjacency matrices and lists), the number of length-kkk walks ending at each node is the vector (A⊤)k1(A^\top)^k \mathbf{1}(A⊤)k1. Katz centrality sums them with geometric weights:

    x=∑k=1∞βk(A⊤)k1  =  ((I−βA⊤)−1−I)1.x = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \beta^k (A^\top)^k \mathbf{1} \;=\; \left( (I - \beta A^\top)^{-1} - I \right) \mathbf{1} .x=k=1∑∞​βk(A⊤)k1=((I−βA⊤)−1−I)1.

    The series converges if and only if β<1/λmax⁡\beta < 1/\lambda_{\max}β<1/λmax​, where λmax⁡\lambda_{\max}λmax​ is the largest eigenvalue of AAA. Rearranging gives a recursive form that shows the connection to eigenvector centrality:

    x(v)=β∑u→v(x(u)+1).x(v) = \beta \sum_{u \to v} \bigl( x(u) + 1 \bigr) .x(v)=βu→v∑​(x(u)+1).

    Each in-link contributes its source’s score plus one. The “+1” is the whole difference from eigenvector centrality: every node passes on at least a baseline, so nodes outside the dominant strongly connected component no longer vanish.

    The two limits

    • As β→0\beta \to 0β→0, walks of length 1 dominate, and the ranking converges to in-degree.
    • As β→1/λmax⁡\beta \to 1/\lambda_{\max}β→1/λmax​, long walks dominate, the series grows without bound, and the ranking converges to eigenvector centrality.

    Between the limits, β\betaβ is a dial between local popularity and global, recursive prestige. Bonacich’s alpha centrality and PageRank both belong to the same family, differing in what they add to each node and whether they divide by out-degree.

    Axioms

    Katz satisfies density and score monotonicity, and the kkk half of the size axiom (see Axioms for centrality). Adding a link x→yx \to yx→y adds new walks into yyy and removes none, so yyy’s score always rises. In that respect Katz behaves better than eigenvector centrality and as well as PageRank.

    Computing it

    The recursive form gives an iteration: start from x=0x = 0x=0 and repeat x←βA⊤(x+1)x \leftarrow \beta A^\top (x + \mathbf{1})x←βA⊤(x+1). Each step costs one pass over the arcs and the error shrinks by a factor of about βλmax⁡\beta \lambda_{\max}βλmax​ per step. Alternatively, solve the sparse linear system (I−βA⊤)x=βA⊤1(I - \beta A^\top) x = \beta A^\top \mathbf{1}(I−βA⊤)x=βA⊤1 with Jacobi or Gauss–Seidel iterations, as for PageRank (see PageRank as a linear system).

    The trouble with β on the web

    The admissible range of β\betaβ depends on λmax⁡\lambda_{\max}λmax​, and on a web graph that number is set by its densest corner. The spectral radius of a graph is at least the smallest in-degree inside any of its subgraphs, counting only arcs within the subgraph. One cluster of a few thousand domains that all link to each other therefore pushes λmax⁡\lambda_{\max}λmax​ into the thousands and forces β\betaβ below 1/10001/10001/1000 for the whole graph. With β\betaβ that small, Katz centrality is in-degree plus a rounding error everywhere outside the cluster. On a graph full of link farms, the farms decide the parameter. PageRank avoids this by dividing each node’s score among its out-links, which caps the spectral radius of its matrix at 1.

    Where it is used

    Katz centrality and its relative, the Katz index between pairs of nodes, are popular in social network analysis and in link prediction, where the weighted count of walks between two nodes predicts future links between them. In web ranking it is mostly of historical and conceptual importance: it showed in 1953 how to count indirect endorsements with decay, an idea PageRank refined forty-five years later.
