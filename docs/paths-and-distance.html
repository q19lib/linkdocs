                Paths, distance and reachability

                How many clicks separate two sites, and whether one can reach the other at all. Breadth-first search answers both, and geometric centralities are built on the answer.

                A path from uuu to vvv is a sequence of arcs that starts at uuu, follows links, and ends at vvv. The distance d(u,v)d(u, v)d(u,v) is the length of the shortest such path, counted in arcs. On the web it is the minimum number of clicks a reader needs to get from one site to another. If no path exists, vvv is unreachable from uuu and d(u,v)=∞d(u, v) = \inftyd(u,v)=∞.

                Distances matter because a family of rankings, the geometric centralities, score a node by how close the rest of the graph is to it: closeness-centrality, harmonic-centrality and betweenness-centrality, which counts how many shortest paths pass through a node.

                Distance is not symmetric

                In a directed graph d(u,v)d(u, v)d(u,v) and d(v,u)d(v, u)d(v,u) can differ, and one can be infinite while the other is not. In the running example, d(E,G)=2d(\text{E}, \text{G}) = 2d(E,G)=2 through C, but d(G,E)=∞d(\text{G}, \text{E}) = \inftyd(G,E)=∞: G links nowhere and nothing links to E.

                Here is every distance in the running example. Row uuu, column vvv gives d(u,v)d(u, v)d(u,v); a dash means unreachable.

                from \ to A B C D E F G
                A 0 1 1 – – – 2
                B 2 0 1 – – – 2
                C 1 2 0 – – – 1
                D 1 2 1 0 – – 2
                E 2 3 1 1 0 1 2
                F – – – – – 0 –
                G – – – – – – 0

                Most of the table is dashes. Unreachable pairs are the norm in web graphs, not an edge case, and any measure built on distance must decide what to do with them. closeness-centrality handles them badly and harmonic-centrality handles them well, which is the main reason the second replaced the first.

                Reachability

                The set of nodes reachable from uuu is its forward ball of infinite radius; the set of nodes that can reach uuu is its backward ball. Reachability decomposes a graph into strongly connected components: groups in which every node reaches every other. On the web those components arrange themselves into the The bow-tie structure shape.

                In the running example, E reaches everything, while F and G reach only themselves. Column C is full except for F and G: every node that links anywhere eventually reaches C.

                Breadth-first search

                Breadth-first search (BFS) computes all distances from one source in time proportional to the number of arcs it touches:

                dist[s] = 0; queue = [s]
while queue not empty:
    u = queue.pop_front()
    for v in successors(u):
        if dist[v] is unset:
            dist[v] = dist[u] + 1
            queue.push_back(v)

                BFS visits nodes in order of distance, so the first time it reaches vvv it has found a shortest path. Each arc is examined once, so one BFS costs O(n+m)O(n + m)O(n+m). Running BFS on the transposed graph (see Adjacency matrices and lists) gives the distances to sss from everywhere, which is what in-link-based centralities need.

                All-pairs distances are out of reach

                A geometric centrality needs the distances between all pairs, which naively means one BFS per node: O(n(n+m))O(n(n + m))O(n(n+m)). For Common Crawl’s domain graph, with n≈1.2×108n \approx 1.2 \times 10^8n≈1.2×108 and m≈2.5×109m \approx 2.5 \times 10^9m≈2.5×109, that is about 3×10173 \times 10^{17}3×1017 arc visits. At a billion visits per second it would take nearly ten years.

                Two ideas make geometric centralities feasible at that scale anyway:

                • Approximate counting. You rarely need every distance, only how many nodes lie within distance ttt for each ttt. HyperBall estimates those counts for every node at once with HyperLogLog counters, in a few dozen passes over the graph. Common Crawl computes harmonic-centrality this way.
                • Sampling. Run BFS from a random sample of sources and extrapolate. This is how large-scale betweenness is usually estimated (see brandes-algorithm).

                Diameter and typical distance

                The diameter is the largest finite distance between any two nodes. It is a fragile statistic: one long chain of pages sets it. Network scientists prefer the distance distribution, the fraction of reachable pairs at each distance, and the effective diameter, the distance within which 90% of reachable pairs lie. Web graphs and social networks have small typical distances, the small-world property, and the neighbourhood-function entry shows how to measure the whole distribution at scale. On Facebook’s 2011 friendship graph, 721 million users were on average 4.74 friendships apart, a figure computed with HyperANF.

                Weighted distance

                When arcs carry lengths, BFS gives way to Dijkstra’s algorithm, which costs O(m+nlog⁡n)O(m + n \log n)O(m+nlogn) with a good priority queue. Link graphs rarely use weighted distances; the meaningful weights on links, such as how much importance they transfer, enter through random-walks instead.
