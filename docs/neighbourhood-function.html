    Network structure

    The neighbourhood function

    For each distance t, how many pairs of nodes are within t links of each other. From it come the distance distribution, the average distance and the effective diameter, the robust ways to say how "small" a graph is.

    The neighbourhood function N(t)N(t)N(t) of a graph counts the ordered pairs of nodes (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) with d(x,y)≤td(x, y) \le td(x,y)≤t: the pairs within ttt links of each other. It summarises the whole distance structure of a graph in one increasing curve, and its derivatives give the statistics that network scientists actually use to compare graphs: the distribution of distances, the average distance and the effective diameter. Its plot is the hop plot of Faloutsos, Faloutsos and Faloutsos’s 1999 study of the internet’s topology; Palmer, Gibbons and Faloutsos showed how to approximate it for large graphs; HyperANF made the approximation fast enough for billion-node graphs.

    where B(x,t)B(x, t)B(x,t) is the ball of radius ttt around xxx. N(0)=nN(0) = nN(0)=n, since every node reaches itself, and N(t)N(t)N(t) stops growing once ttt passes the longest finite distance.

    From it:

    • Distance distribution. The share of reachable pairs at distance exactly ttt: (N(t)−N(t−1))/(N(∞)−n)\bigl(N(t) - N(t-1)\bigr) / \bigl(N(\infty) - n\bigr)(N(t)−N(t−1))/(N(∞)−n).
    • Average distance over the reachable pairs.
    • Effective diameter. The distance within which 90% of reachable pairs lie, interpolated between integers. Unlike the diameter, the largest finite distance, it does not depend on a few freak chains.
    • Spid, the shortest-path index of dispersion: the variance of the distance distribution divided by its mean. Boldi, Rosa and Vigna observed that social networks tend to have spid below 1 and web graphs above 1, a cheap way to tell the two kinds of graph apart.

    Worked example

    The running example (see the distance table in paths and distance) has 19 ordered pairs of distinct nodes with a directed path:

    0 7 (each node to itself) 7
    1 10 (the arcs) 17 52.6%
    2 8 25 94.7%

    The average distance is (10⋅1+8⋅2+1⋅3)/19=1.53(10 \cdot 1 + 8 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 3)/19 = 1.53(10⋅1+8⋅2+1⋅3)/19=1.53, and the effective diameter, interpolating where 90% is reached, is about 1.9. The diameter is 3. Of the 42 ordered pairs of distinct nodes, 23 have no path at all, which is why every statistic above is computed over reachable pairs only.

    Computing it at scale

    Exact computation needs all distances, a breadth-first search from every node. ANF and its successors estimate every ball size ∣B(x,t)∣|B(x, t)|∣B(x,t)∣ with a probabilistic counter per node and grow all balls one step per pass over the graph (see HyperBall). Summing the estimates gives N(t)N(t)N(t) with a relative error close to that of the individual counters, in as many passes as the graph’s longest shortest path. HyperANF computed the neighbourhood function of Facebook’s 2011 friendship graph, 721 million nodes, giving the famous average distance of 4.74.

    What it reveals

    • How small the world is. Average distance and effective diameter quantify the small-world property and allow graphs of different sizes to be compared.
    • Change over time. Leskovec, Kleinberg and Faloutsos tracked real networks as they grew and found that effective diameters shrink while the graphs densify, the number of arcs growing faster than the number of nodes. Both contradict the assumptions of earlier random-graph models.
    • Crawl health. A crawl that breaks URL normalisation or stops following some link type produces a neighbourhood function with a long tail or an abrupt plateau. Comparing successive crawls’ curves is a quick diagnostic.
    • Robustness. Removing nodes in order of some centrality and recomputing N(t)N(t)N(t) shows which nodes hold the graph together. Boldi, Rosa and Vigna used this to compare removal strategies and found that social networks and web graphs react very differently to losing their most central nodes.

    Why it matters for rankings

    Harmonic centrality is the per-node version of the same information: h(x)=∑t(∣B(x,t)∣−∣B(x,t−1)∣)/th(x) = \sum_t \bigl(|B(x,t)| - |B(x,t-1)|\bigr)/th(x)=∑t​(∣B(x,t)∣−∣B(x,t−1)∣)/t for balls built from in-links. A graph whose distance distribution is concentrated at 3 or 4 steps gives almost every well-connected node a similar harmonic value, because most of the graph sits at the same few distances from each of them. The flatness of Common Crawl’s harmonic values near the top (see ranks and scores) is the distance distribution showing through.
