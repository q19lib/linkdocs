    PageRank in depth

    Push algorithms

    Compute personalized PageRank by pushing probability outward from the seed, one node at a time, and stopping when the leftovers are small. The work depends on the precision you want, not on the size of the graph.

    Power iteration touches every arc of the graph in every pass, even when you only care about the neighbourhood of one node. Push algorithms do the opposite. To compute Personalized PageRank from a seed, they keep a little probability mass at the seed and repeatedly push it outward to neighbours, settling part of it at each node they visit. When the unsettled mass left anywhere is below a threshold, they stop. The total work depends only on the threshold and the damping factor, so the algorithm answers local questions on a billion-arc graph while touching a few thousand arcs. Pavel Berkhin’s bookmark-coloring algorithm and Andersen, Chung and Lang’s local push, both from 2006, are the classic forms.

    Forward push

    Keep two vectors: an estimate ppp, initially zero, and a residual rrr, initially 1 at the seed sss and 0 elsewhere. A push at node uuu does three things:

    1. move a (1−α)(1 - \alpha)(1−α) share of the residual into the estimate: p(u)+=(1−α) r(u)p(u) \mathrel{+}= (1 - \alpha)\, r(u)p(u)+=(1−α)r(u);
    2. spread the remaining α r(u)\alpha\, r(u)αr(u) equally over uuu’s out-neighbours’ residuals (from a dangling node, send it back to the seed);
    3. set r(u)=0r(u) = 0r(u)=0.

    Repeat pushes at any node whose residual is at least ε⋅d+(u)\varepsilon \cdot d^+(u)ε⋅d+(u), in any order, until none is left. The key invariant is that the exact answer equals the estimate plus the personalized PageRank of the residual:

    ps=p+∑ur(u) pu,p_s = p + \sum_u r(u)\, p_u ,ps​=p+u∑​r(u)pu​,

    so the error at the end is at most the total residual. Each push settles at least (1−α) ε d+(u)(1 - \alpha)\, \varepsilon\, d^+(u)(1−α)εd+(u) of mass at a cost of d+(u)d^+(u)d+(u) operations, and at most 1 unit of mass exists, so the total work is at most 1/((1−α) ε)1/((1-\alpha)\,\varepsilon)1/((1−α)ε), independent of nnn and mmm.

    Local clustering

    Andersen, Chung and Lang used push to find communities. Compute an approximate personalized PageRank from a seed, sort nodes by p(u)/d(u)p(u)/d(u)p(u)/d(u), and scan the prefixes of that order for the set with the lowest conductance, the ratio of arcs leaving the set to arcs inside it. They proved that this finds a cluster of near-optimal conductance around the seed in time proportional to the cluster’s size, not the graph’s. The method underlies many local community-detection tools (see Community detection).

    Backward push

    Forward push answers “where does a walk from sss go?” Reversing the arcs answers “where do walks that reach ttt come from?” Backward push starts with residual 1 at a target ttt and pushes along in-links, estimating the contribution of every node to ttt’s PageRank. Andersen, Borgs, Chayes, Hopcroft, Mirrokni and Teng used it to compute the supporting set of a page: the nodes responsible for most of its PageRank. A page whose PageRank comes overwhelmingly from a small, densely interlinked supporting set is a classic Link spam signature, the same intuition Spam mass formalises with a trusted core.

    Bidirectional estimators

    Combining a backward push from the target with a few forward random walks from the source estimates a single personalized PageRank value ps(t)p_s(t)ps​(t) much faster than either method alone. Lofgren, Banerjee, Goel and Seshadhri’s FAST-PPR (2014) and the later BiPPR exploit this: the backward push builds a “frontier” of nodes that are close to ttt, and the forward walks only need to hit that frontier. Such estimators make it practical to answer “how relevant is ttt to sss?” on demand for arbitrary pairs.

    Global PageRank by push

    Pushing from a uniform residual instead of a single seed computes ordinary PageRank. Choosing the node with the largest residual at each step is the Gauss–Southwell method, which spends effort where the remaining error is largest. For global rankings it rarely beats well-tuned Gauss–Seidel sweeps over a compressed graph, because sweeps read memory sequentially while pushes jump around (see PageRank at scale). Push methods shine when the question is local.
